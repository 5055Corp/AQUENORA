AQENORA: an online store builder for African sellers and the diaspora

Is there room for a Shopify-style platform built for Africa, who already competes there, how payments work, and a realistic plan to build it with open-source software on less than $500.

Key takeaways
  1. The market is growing, but nobody agrees how big it is. Estimates for African e-commerce in 2025 range from about $40.5B (Statista) to $360.7B (IMARC), a gap of nearly 9× [1][2]. Most purchases happen on phones [1].
  2. Shopify has a real gap in Africa. Its built-in payment system isn't available in Nigeria, so sellers bolt on another payment provider and pay Shopify's extra transaction fee. Plans are also billed in dollars [6][7].
  3. But the local market is crowded and cheap. Paystack Storefront is free, and Bumpa, Catlog, QShop, Selar and Flutterwave Store already serve this market [3][4][5]. "Shopify for Africa" alone isn't enough to stand out.
  4. Suggested angle: sell locally and abroad from one store, set up in plain language. Reviewers describe the local tools as mostly focused on one country, and Shopify as the default for selling internationally [4][8]. AQENORA can aim to combine both, with setup simpler than either (my inference).
  5. A no-coder on $500 can't safely build a full platform in one go. Build in phases: first prove demand by setting up stores by hand, then build a small first version on Medusa, a free open-source e-commerce engine, with Paystack for payments.

2. Where Shopify falls short for African sellers

The opening
  • No built-in payments. Shopify Payments isn't available in Nigeria. Sellers need a third-party provider and pay an extra 0.5–2% Shopify fee on top [6].
  • Costs in dollars, plus apps. Plans start at $29–39/month, and most stores spend $100+ more per month on apps [6][8].
  • Getting paid from abroad is hard. Stripe isn't officially available to Nigerians, and PayPal works poorly for receiving payments [7].
  • Built for setting up on a laptop. One comparison calls Shopify "desktop-first for setup", while Bumpa runs the whole store from a phone [8].

AQENORA positioning (my inference)

"Your store, ready in 10 minutes. Get paid in naira, cedis, rand, CFA, dollars or euros."

  • Who it's for: African brands (fashion, beauty, art, food products) selling at home and to the diaspora.
  • How it's different: local and international card payments from one checkout, priced in local currency, with a guided setup in plain language.
  • Where to start: Nigeria and Ghana, where Paystack's flat rates make costs easy to explain. Add South Africa and Côte d'Ivoire next. Paystack supports both [12].

Flutterwave operates in about 35 countries [16], which makes it useful for Francophone West Africa. Its fee for international cards is reported inconsistently. Flutterwave's own notice says it rose from 3.8% to 4.8% [15], but several 2026 reviews still quote 3.8% [16]. Use 4.8% when you plan, and check before launch.

Your easiest way to earn: split payments. Paystack can automatically send most of each payment to the seller and keep a commission for the platform. Paystack calls these sellers "subaccounts". AQENORA would never hold sellers' money, which lowers your legal and compliance burden. Confirm the details with Paystack's documentation.

5. How AQENORA makes money

Suggested pricing (to test with sellers)
  • Starter, free: 1 store, AQENORA subdomain, 2% platform commission per sale through the payment split. This undercuts Selar's 5% [3].
  • Pro, a monthly fee in local currency: your own domain, 0.5% commission, international checkout, all themes.
  • Why both: the free plan competes with Paystack Storefront. Pro is far cheaper than Shopify plus apps [8].

6. "Easier than Shopify's guides": product principles

These are built into the prototype
  1. One question per screen during setup. Ask for things in the order a seller thinks of them: what you sell, your shop name, your first product, how you get paid.
  2. A visible "Get to your first sale" checklist on the home screen, so the next step is always clear.
  3. Plain-language help. Every technical term has a one-line "What does this mean?" next to it, rather than a link to a help center.
  4. Phone first. Every task can be done one-handed on a budget Android phone, matching the 60%+ mobile share [1].
  5. Simple theme editor. Pick a look, colour and font, and preview it live. No code and no drag-and-drop grid.

7. Build plan on less than $500

Open-source route, in phases

An honest warning first. A platform that hosts many sellers' stores and handles their money is much harder than a single app. Security, uptime and payment errors affect other people's income. With no coding background, use AI coding assistants, keep the first version tiny, and look for a technical co-founder once early sellers show demand.

Phases

Phase 0 · Month 1
Set up stores by hand ($0). Find 10 sellers on Instagram and WhatsApp. Build their stores yourself on existing free tools and note every question they ask; those questions become your setup steps. Check that at least 3 would pay.
Phase 1 · Months 2–5
First version. Medusa backend with one storefront template, a web address per seller (seller.aqenora.com), Paystack payment splits, and the setup flow and dashboard from the prototype.
Phase 2 · Months 6–9
Expansion. Theme editor, sellers' own domains, Flutterwave for Francophone markets, pricing in USD/EUR for diaspora buyers, and the paid Pro plan.
Phase 3 · Month 10+
Seller mobile app. Reuse the Expo plan from your first guide as the seller's phone app.
